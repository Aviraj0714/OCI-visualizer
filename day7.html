🗃 Why Databases Exist & the Relational Model

⏱ 30 minFoundationalExam Weight: Medium
Learning Objectives
  • Distinguish data from information, and explain why structure matters at scale.
  • Identify tables, rows, columns, schemas, primary keys, foreign keys, and constraints in a live example.
  • Add a row to a table and a related row in a child table, and observe the relationship enforced.
Prerequisite: none — this is lesson one
📚
Real-World Analogy

A spreadsheet of loose notes is data. A library's card catalog — author, title, shelf location, cross-referenced by subject — is information: the same facts, structured so you can actually find and trust them. A relational database is that card catalog, enforced by software.

🏢
Business Use Case

A bank cannot allow a "Transactions" record to reference a customer that does not exist. A foreign key constraint from Transactions to Customers makes that structurally impossible — not a matter of hoping the application code got it right.

A table stores one kind of thing (Customers, Orders). Each row is one instance; each column is one attribute. A schema is the structural blueprint. A primary key uniquely identifies a row; a foreign key in one table references a primary key in another, enforcing the relationship between them.

Exam Tip

A foreign key constraint is enforced by the database engine itself, not the application — this is what "referential integrity" means, and it holds even if a careless script tries to insert a bad row directly.

⚠
Common Trap

A primary key guarantees uniqueness within its own table; it says nothing about other tables. Only a foreign key constraint links two tables together.

📋 Normalization

⏱ 25 minFoundationalExam Weight: Medium
Learning Objectives
  • Transform an unnormalized table through 1NF, 2NF, and 3NF.
Prerequisite: Indexes
Unnormalized
1NF
2NF
3NF
Unnormalized

One row per order, with repeating comma-separated product columns. Hard to query, hard to keep consistent.

1NF — Atomic Values

Split repeating groups into their own rows — one product per row. Every cell now holds a single value.

2NF — Remove Partial Dependencies

Split out attributes that depend on only part of a composite key (e.g. product price depends on product, not on the order) into their own table.

3NF — Remove Transitive Dependencies

Split out attributes that depend on a non-key attribute (e.g. customer city depends on customer, not directly on the order) into their own table.

🎯
Exam Tip

Higher normal forms reduce data duplication and update anomalies but can require more JOINs at query time — real schemas often deliberately denormalize specific tables for read performance.

⚡ OLTP

Many short transactions: checkout, deposit, booking. Optimized for fast, concurrent writes on normalized schemas.

📊 OLAP

Complex analytical queries over huge historical datasets. Optimized for read-heavy aggregation, often denormalized (star schema).

🌐 Availability Domains & Fault Domains

⏱ 25 minCore ConceptExam Weight: Very High
Learning Objectives
  • Distinguish Availability Domains (data-center-scale) from Fault Domains (rack-scale) within an OCI region.
Prerequisite: Single Point of Failure
🏢
Real-World Analogy

Availability Domains are separate buildings on separate power grids across a city — a citywide event affecting one building does not take down the others. Fault Domains are separate racks within one building — one rack losing power does not take down the rest of that building.

Exam Tip

Not every OCI region has 3 Availability Domains — some regions have only 1. Fault Domains (always 3, within a single AD) exist even in single-AD regions, so you can still design for rack-level resilience anywhere.

Common Trap

Spreading instances across Fault Domains protects against rack-level failure, not data-center-level failure. Only spreading across Availability Domains protects against a whole-AD outage.

⚠ What is Disaster Recovery

⏱ 20 minFoundationalExam Weight: High
Learning Objectives
  • Name the disaster categories DR planning must cover and why business continuity depends on it.
Prerequisite: Module 2 complete
🏚
Real-World Analogy

HA keeps your house standing through a strong storm. DR is having a second house, in a different city, ready to move into if the first one burns down entirely. They solve different classes of problems.

Disaster Recovery plans for events that HA alone cannot absorb: a natural disaster taking out a data center, a cyber attack corrupting data at the source, a power failure beyond redundant supply, or a full region failure. The goal is business continuity — the organization keeps operating, even in degraded form.

⏱ RTO & RPO

⏱ 35 minCore ConceptExam Weight: Very High
Learning Objectives
  • Define Recovery Time Objective and Recovery Point Objective precisely.
  • Choose a DR strategy that satisfies a given RTO/RPO pair.
Prerequisite: What is Disaster Recovery
⏱
Real-World Analogy

RTO answers "how long can the store stay closed?" RPO answers "how much of today's sales ledger are we willing to lose and re-enter by hand?" They are independent business decisions, not technical constants.

Recovery Time Objective (RTO)

Maximum acceptable time to restore service after a disaster.

RTO: 4h
Recovery Point Objective (RPO)

Maximum acceptable data loss, measured in time.

RPO: 4h
Exercise › Which DR Strategy Fits?

A bank requires RTO < 5 minutes and RPO near zero. Which topology fits?

Cold Standby
Pilot Light
Active-Active
🎯
Exam Tip

Lower RTO/RPO always costs more — near-zero RPO typically requires synchronous replication, which has a latency/distance cost. Certification scenarios test whether you can match business requirements to the cheapest strategy that still satisfies them.

⚠
Common Trap

RTO and RPO are independent dials. A short RTO does not imply a short RPO, and vice versa — you can recover fast but still lose an hour of data, or recover slowly with near-zero data loss.

OCI Full Stack Disaster Recovery

Architecture · Protection Groups · DR Plans · Switchover, Failover & Failback

🏗 Full Stack DR Architecture

⏱ 25 minCore ServiceExam Weight: Very High
Learning Objectives
  • Explain what OCI Full Stack DR orchestrates and why it exists on top of replication and backups.
Prerequisite: Module 3 complete
🎯
Real-World Analogy

Replication moves the data. Full Stack DR is the conductor: it knows the exact order to start every instrument — database first, then app tier, then DNS cutover — so the whole orchestra plays in sync during an actual disaster, not just individually correct notes.

OCI Full Stack Disaster Recovery orchestrates entire application stacks — compute, database, load balancers, DNS — through predefined, testable DR Plans, grouped by Protection Groups, executed via Switchover (planned) or Failover (unplanned), and returned to normal via Failback.

Exam Tip

Full Stack DR does not replicate data itself — it orchestrates and sequences the recovery of resources whose replication is handled by underlying services (database replication, Object Storage replication, etc.).
